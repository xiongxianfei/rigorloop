# Zave and Jackson Four Dark Corners of Requirements Engineering

## Key takeaway

A claim about the real-world outcome depends on both the specified behavior and relevant environmental assumptions.

## Summary

This theoretical paper distinguishes requirements, specifications and domain knowledge. REM uses that distinction to inspect the reasoning from a desired result to implementable obligations, without adopting the paper’s formalism or treating it as a complete stakeholder-engagement method.

Specifications and domain knowledge jointly support requirement satisfaction. Some assumptions are probable rather than invariant. This supplies a conditional argument, not empirical confirmation that a particular environment satisfies its assumptions or a prescription of REM cardinalities.

- **C1 — Conditional satisfaction:** §5 relates domain knowledge and implementable specifications to required outcomes.
- **C2 — Assumption limits:** §5.4’s probable-assumption passage is on printed manuscript p. 25 (PDF page 26). Conditional assurance cannot establish that the assumption holds in a particular environment.
- **C3 — Boundary matters:** §6.1 distinguishes shared and unshared phenomena and machine/environment control. REM uses that distinction to inspect what a proposed system can observe and control.

## Contribution and independence
This is an independent theoretical argument, not a replication of NASA’s handbook and not empirical evidence that one particular REM schema works. Its treatment of formal aspects deliberately does not cover all social aspects of requirements work.

## What it does not establish
It does not prescribe three requirement levels, a universal requirement-parent count, a database representation or a way to infer stakeholder agreement from a completed analysis. It does not establish that IR, SR and AR are exact synonyms for its terminology.
